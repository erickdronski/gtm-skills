# Objections to a business case, and what a real answer looks like

The objections below are the ones that actually get raised, roughly in the order
they appear as a case moves from a champion to finance to procurement. For each:
what is really being asked, and what a defensible answer sounds like.

The general principle: every one of these is easier to answer *before* it is
asked. A case that pre-empts three of them earns the benefit of the doubt on the
rest.

## "Where did that number come from?"

**Really asking:** is this measured or invented?

The assumption ledger answers this before it is asked, which is most of why it
exists. If the answer to this question requires you to reconstruct reasoning on
the spot, the case was not ready.

> "Ticket volume is your Zendesk export for calendar 2025 — Dana sent it on the
> 14th. Cost per ticket is derived: your loaded agent cost of $82k divided by
> 6,400 tickets per agent-year, both from your Q2 operating review. The
> deflection rate is the one number we have not measured; that is an assumption
> and it is bounded."

## "Your assumptions are too aggressive."

**Really asking:** I do not trust the number and I want you to move it.

Do not defend the point estimate. Go to the range.

> "Possibly. That is why it is bounded at 10 to 24 percent rather than stated as
> a fact. At the low bound the case is negative — that is the real decision
> here, and it is why I would rather measure it than argue about it. A two-week
> sample of your top ten ticket categories would settle it."

Offering to measure the disputed input converts an argument into a plan, and it
is nearly always the right move.

## "What if we do nothing?"

**Really asking:** is the status quo actually a problem?

If you cannot answer this, there is no case. The answer must be in the buyer's
terms and tied to a deadline or a trend, not to your product.

## "This assumes headcount reduction we will never make."

**Really asking:** you have modeled savings we will not take.

The best response is to have already handled it in the model.

> "Agreed, and the model does not assume it. Realization on the handle-time
> driver is 0.7 precisely because you have committed to absorbing growth rather
> than reducing headcount — the value shows up as volume handled without adding
> the two heads the growth plan implies."

## "Your ROI is higher than anything we have ever achieved."

**Really asking:** this is not credible.

A suspiciously high ROI is a liability, not an asset. If the model produces one,
check realization factors before defending it — an implausible result is usually
a modeling error rather than an exceptional product.

> "It is high, and I would rather it were defensible than impressive. The floor
> case is the number I would plan against."

## "What does this not include?"

**Really asking:** what are you hiding?

Have the answer ready and volunteer it before the question. Typically:
opportunity cost of internal effort beyond what is modeled, ongoing
administration, risk of adoption below plan, and any benefit you deliberately
left out.

Naming a benefit you *excluded* is unusually effective here, because it
demonstrates the model was built to be right rather than to be large.

## "Why this discount rate?"

**Really asking:** did you pick a rate that flatters the case?

> "We used 12% because that is what your finance team gave us. At 15% the NPV is
> still positive and payback moves by about two months — the conclusion does not
> turn on the rate."

Always test the conclusion at a higher rate before the meeting. If it flips,
that is the finding.

## "Someone else claims a bigger number."

**Really asking:** are you being conservative or are you weak?

> "Probably. Most of the difference will be in what each model assumes about
> realization. Ours haircuts time-savings by 30% because it does not convert to
> cash on your current staffing plan. If you want to compare them properly, the
> comparison to make is at the assumption level, not the headline."

Inviting an assumption-level comparison is a strong position when your
assumptions are the more conservative ones, and it reframes a headline contest
into a methodology contest you can win.

## "This is a three-year case and we budget annually."

**Really asking:** what happens in year one?

Know the year-one number cold, including whether it is negative. Many good
investments are negative in year one; saying so plainly and showing where the
crossover falls is far better than being caught by the question.

## "Procurement will want to see the internal cost."

**Really asking:** you have modeled only your fees.

Have it already in the model. Customer-side effort — content authoring, program
management, integration work — belongs in the cost lines. Omitting it is the
fastest way to lose a case at this stage, and including it costs less
credibility than it buys.

## "We already have a tool that does this."

**Really asking:** why is the incremental spend justified?

This is a positioning problem surfacing inside a financial conversation. The
model should compare against the *current* state including the existing tool,
not against nothing. If the existing tool's contract can be retired, that
becomes a cost-avoided driver and often carries the case on its own.

## When the honest answer is that the case does not clear

Say it. The alternatives are: change the scope, change the price, change the
timeline, or find a different driver.

> "At this scope it does not pay back inside three years. It does if we start
> with the two highest-volume categories instead of all of them — that is a
> smaller deployment, and the case clears in 14 months."

A seller who says this is in a category of one, and it is remembered long after
the deal.
