# Positioning

Positioning is context-setting. It is the answer to a question the buyer asks
before any of your features matter: *what kind of thing is this, and compared to
what?*

Get it wrong and every downstream asset works against you. Buyers evaluate you
on criteria that belong to a different category, your strengths read as
irrelevant, and your team writes increasingly elaborate copy to compensate for a
frame that was wrong at the start.

The single most useful reframe: **you do not position against a competitor, you
position against the alternative** — which is very often a spreadsheet, an
intern, or doing nothing at all.

## The sequence

This follows April Dunford's method, which is the most reliable framework
available for this, and its steps must run in order. Each one constrains the
next, and skipping ahead to "what makes us different" without first establishing
"different from what" is the failure mode this sequence exists to prevent.

### 1. Competitive alternatives

What would the customer do if you did not exist? Not "who are our competitors" —
what would actually happen. Interrogate this hard, because it is where the real
information is:

- A named competitor
- An in-house build or an existing internal process
- A spreadsheet, a shared doc, a person doing it manually
- Nothing — living with the problem

"Nothing" is the most common alternative and the hardest to beat, because it has
no procurement cycle and no switching cost. If it is the primary alternative,
the entire positioning has to be about the cost of inaction, and messaging built
around beating a named competitor will miss.

Ask the user which of these actually shows up in lost deals. If they do not
know, that is the first research task, and it is worth more than the rest of
this exercise.

### 2. Unique attributes

What do you have that the alternatives do not? Capabilities, not adjectives.
"Powerful", "intuitive", and "enterprise-grade" are things every competitor also
claims, which means they carry zero information.

The test: could a competitor say this sentence about themselves without lying?
If yes, delete it. What survives is usually shorter and much more specific than
what the team started with — and that specificity is the entire asset.

### 3. Value those attributes enable

For each surviving attribute, ask "so what" until you reach something the buyer
cares about independent of your product.

*We index the codebase as a graph* → so what → *queries resolve across files* →
so what → *the agent stops making changes that break callers three files away* →
so what → *review time drops and fewer regressions ship*.

Stop at the point where the buyer would nod without needing your product
explained. Going further reaches generic business outcomes — "increase revenue"
— that no longer distinguish you.

### 4. Who cares a lot

Which customers care disproportionately about that value? Not everyone who
*could* buy — the segment for whom this is urgent.

Characteristics that make a segment care disproportionately are almost always
structural: they have the problem at unusual scale, they have been burned by it
recently, or they are regulated into caring. Find the structural reason and the
targeting writes itself.

### 5. Market category

What context makes your value obvious? The category sets buyer expectations —
what they compare you to, what they expect to pay, what checklist they evaluate
against.

Three moves are available:

**Play in an existing category.** Easiest. Buyers already have budget and a
frame. You have to win on their established criteria.

**Position in a subsegment.** "Observability, for agent workloads." Inherits
budget while shifting the comparison set. Usually the right answer.

**Create a category.** Enormously expensive — you fund the education of the
entire market. Correct only when no existing category makes your value legible,
and you have the capital and the years. Most category creation attempts are
subsegment plays that would have worked better labeled honestly.

### 6. Relevant trend, optionally

A trend can make the positioning feel urgent, but only if it is genuinely
connected. Bolting a trend onto positioning it does not fit reads as
opportunistic, and buyers are unusually good at detecting it.

## Turning it into messaging

The positioning is internal. Messaging is what ships. Build the hierarchy in
this order and keep it in one document:

**One-line positioning statement** — internal, precise, not customer-facing:
> For [segment] who [situation], [product] is a [category] that [key value].
> Unlike [primary alternative], it [key differentiator].

**Value pillars** — two to four, each stating a differentiated capability and
the value it enables. Each pillar needs proof: a metric, a customer outcome, a
demo moment. A pillar without proof is a claim, and claims are free.

**Proof points** — the specific evidence under each pillar. This is where the
value case belongs.

**Objection handling** — the three objections that actually kill deals, and the
honest response to each. Not rebuttals; responses. An objection you cannot
answer honestly is a product roadmap item, and pretending otherwise wastes
everyone's time.

## Testing it

Positioning is testable, and most teams never test it:

- **Sales call test.** Does a rep get further with it than without? The fastest
  signal available.
- **The "compared to what" test.** Read the copy to someone unfamiliar. Ask what
  they think the alternative is. If they cannot say, the positioning is not
  doing its job.
- **The competitor test.** Take your homepage headline, swap in a competitor's
  logo. Does it still read as true? If so, you have written a category
  description rather than a position.
- **The lost-deal test.** Do lost deals cite reasons your positioning claims to
  address? If so, the positioning is not reaching them, or is not true.

## Common failures

**Positioning for the team instead of the buyer.** Internal language, internal
architecture, internal priorities. Buyers do not care how it works until they
care what it does.

**Leading with the category you wish you were in.** If buyers put you in a
category, you are in it. Fighting the frame from inside it never works;
subsegmenting does.

**Feature lists as positioning.** A list of capabilities tells the buyer nothing
about whether it is for them.

**Changing it every quarter.** Positioning compounds. Frequent changes reset the
compounding to zero and confuse everyone who was starting to understand you.

**Positioning against a competitor buyers have never heard of.** If the buyer
does not know the competitor, the contrast is invisible and you have spent your
best sentence on nothing.
