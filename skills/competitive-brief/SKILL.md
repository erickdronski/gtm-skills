# Competitive brief

A battlecard has one job: help a seller handle sixty seconds of a live
conversation without lying. Most battlecards fail at this because they are
written as marketing artifacts — every competitor weak, every one of our
features a differentiator — and experienced sellers stop reading them because
using them gets them caught.

The reliable test for whether a battlecard is any good: **would a rep still
trust it after a prospect pushed back on one of its claims?** That standard
forces honest weaknesses, which is what makes the rest of the card credible.

## Sourcing

Rank sources by how much they should move your conclusions:

1. **Your own win-loss data.** What buyers actually said. Nothing else is close.
2. **Recorded calls where the competitor came up.** Real objections, real
   language.
3. **The competitor's own materials** — docs, pricing, changelog, job postings.
   Public, verifiable, and quotable back to a prospect.
4. **Customer reviews.** Directionally useful, heavily selection-biased.
5. **What your team believes.** Usually a mix of accurate pattern-matching and
   eighteen-month-old information presented as current.

Label which tier each claim came from. A battlecard where every claim reads as
equally certain is one where the reader cannot tell which parts to lean on.

**Date every factual claim about a competitor.** Products change. A card
asserting a gap that was closed two quarters ago will get a rep publicly
corrected by a prospect, which costs more than the card ever earned.

## Structure

Write for the sixty-second window. Front-load, keep it scannable, and put detail
below the fold.

### The one-liner

How to frame this competitor in a single sentence a rep can say out loud. Not
"we're better" — the honest frame:

> They are a strong fit for teams that want a single vendor for everything and
> can accept a longer implementation. We win where the team needs to be live in
> weeks and cares about depth in one area.

That sentence concedes real ground, which is precisely why the next thing the
rep says gets believed.

### Where we genuinely win

Two or three, each with proof and each specific enough to be checkable. "Faster"
is not a claim. "Live in 3 weeks versus their published 8-to-12-week
implementation" is.

### Where they genuinely win

The section that makes the card trustworthy, and the one most often deleted.
Include it. A rep who knows the real gaps can navigate to ground where they are
strong; a rep who has been told there are no gaps gets ambushed and improvises
badly.

For each, note whether it is on the roadmap and whether the rep may say so.

### Trap-setting questions

Questions that surface the competitor's structural weaknesses without naming
them. Structural is the operative word — architectural or business-model
constraints they cannot quickly fix, not a feature they might ship next month.

> "How are you planning to handle it when the volume triples during peak? What
> does that do to your costs on their pricing model?"

The question is legitimate on its own terms, which is what separates a good trap
question from a cheap one. If the prospect's honest answer is "that's fine",
the question has cost you nothing.

### Landmines they set for us

What the competitor says about *you*, and the honest response. Reps encounter
this constantly and improvise without it.

Format each as: what they say → whether it is true → what to say. The middle
column matters. Some of what a competitor says about you is accurate, and a rep
who knows which parts can address them directly instead of denying something the
prospect can verify.

### Pricing posture

How they price, where they discount, what their contract typically looks like.
Not a number to quote — postures are durable, prices are not, and a stale price
is worse than none.

## Handling the competitor in conversation

Three rules that hold across almost every situation:

**Never disparage.** Trashing a competitor makes the buyer defensive about a
decision they may have already partly made, and it makes you look worried. Frame
as fit, not as quality.

**Do not chase feature parity.** "We have that too" concedes the frame that the
competitor's feature set is the evaluation criteria. Reframe to the criteria
where you are strong: "Yes — the difference is what happens when X."

**When you lose on fit, say so.** A rep who says "honestly, for what you have
described, they may be the better fit" earns a relationship and often a future
deal. This is not weakness; it is the highest-leverage credibility move
available, and it costs a deal you were going to lose anyway.

## Maintenance

A battlecard is a living document with a decay rate:

- Review quarterly, minimum.
- Re-verify every dated claim before each review.
- Add a line every time the competitor appears in a lost deal.
- Delete claims nobody has used. An unused claim is noise crowding out the three
  that work.

Track which lines reps actually use. The card usually shrinks, and gets better
as it does.

## Common failures

**No honest weaknesses.** Covered above, and the single most common defect.

**Written for marketing, read by nobody.** If it does not fit on one screen, the
rep will not open it during a call, which is the only moment it matters.

**Stale claims presented as current.** Undated assertions about a competitor's
gaps age into liabilities.

**Comparing against their marketing rather than their product.** Their homepage
overstates what they do, exactly as yours does. Compare against the docs.

**One card for every segment.** You may lose to a competitor in enterprise and
beat them in mid-market for structural reasons. One card averaging those cases
is wrong in both.
