# Discovery question bank

Questions organized by qualification element, with what a good answer sounds
like and what a deflection sounds like.

The general craft: **ask about the last time, not the general case.** "Walk me
through the last time this happened" produces specifics with dates and names.
"How do you usually handle this" produces a policy description that may not
reflect what actually happens.

## Pain

> "Walk me through the last time this became a problem. What happened?"

> "What did that cost you — in time, money, or something that slipped?"

> "How long has it been this way?"

> "What happens if nothing changes for another year?"

**Good answer:** a specific incident, with a date, a consequence, and someone
who was unhappy about it.

**Deflection:** "It's an ongoing challenge for us." Follow with "when did it
last come up specifically?" — if there is no specific instance, the pain may be
theoretical.

**The question that decides whether there is a deal:** the "what if nothing
changes" one. If the honest answer is "we carry on", there is no urgency, and
the forecast should say so regardless of how well the rest of the call went.

## Metrics

> "Is this something you're measured on?"

> "What's the number today, and where does it need to be?"

> "Who else sees that number?"

> "If this worked, what would change on your dashboard?"

**Good answer:** a metric they can state without prompting, with a current value
and a target.

**Deflection:** agreeing enthusiastically with a metric *you* proposed. A metric
the seller invented does not survive the buyer's internal review — the champion
has to defend it in a room you are not in.

**Note:** if they cannot produce the current baseline, that is the most useful
finding of the call. Every benefit downstream is unfalsifiable without it, and
offering to help them measure it is often a stronger next step than a demo.

## Economic buyer

> "Who signs off on something this size?"

> "How have similar purchases gone through here before?"

> "What would they need to see from us?"

> "Would it make sense to bring them into the next conversation?"

**Good answer:** a name, a title, and a description of what that person cares
about.

**Deflection:** "I have budget for this." Sometimes true. Follow with "and if it
came in above that, who would you go to?" — the answer names the real economic
buyer.

**The test that matters:** not whether they can name the economic buyer, but
whether you will meet them. Identification without access is the most
over-credited state in pipeline review.

## Decision criteria and process

> "How will you decide between options?"

> "Is there a formal evaluation, or is this more of a judgment call?"

> "Who else needs to weigh in — security, legal, IT, finance?"

> "What's happened with projects like this that didn't get approved here?"

**Good answer:** named stages, named people, rough timelines.

**Deflection:** "We'll just decide as a team." Follow with "who's on that team,
and has everyone seen this yet?"

That last question — what happened to projects that *didn't* get approved — is
the highest-yield question in this whole document. It surfaces the real
approval barrier faster than anything else, and people answer it candidly
because it is about the past rather than about them.

## Paper process

> "Once a decision is made, what happens between then and signature?"

> "Is there a security review? How long did the last one take?"

> "Do you have a standard agreement, or do you work from the vendor's?"

> "Is there a vendor onboarding process I should know about?"

**Good answer:** a described sequence with rough durations, ideally referencing
a specific recent purchase.

**Deflection:** "That part is usually quick." It is usually not. Ask which
vendor they onboarded most recently and how long that took end to end.

Six weeks of security review discovered in the last week of a quarter is the
single most common cause of a slipped enterprise deal, and it is entirely
preventable by asking this in the first two calls.

## Champion

You do not ask someone if they are your champion. You test it.

**Tests, in ascending order of strength:**

- Will they share something internal — an org chart, a requirements doc, the
  current numbers?
- Will they arrange a meeting with someone more senior?
- Will they tell you honestly when something is going badly?
- Will they put their name on a recommendation?

Someone friendly on calls who does none of these is a coach, not a champion. The
distinction shows up at the approval gate, which is the worst place to discover
it.

> "What would you need from me to make the case internally?"

> "Who's likely to push back on this, and what will they say?"

A real champion answers the second question with detail, because they have
already thought about it.

## Competition

> "What else are you looking at?"

> "What would you do if you didn't do anything?"

> "Have you tried to solve this before? What happened?"

**Good answer:** a straight answer, including "nothing yet" or "we're also
talking to X".

**Deflection:** "We're evaluating a few options." Follow with "which ones are
furthest along?"

**Remember the leading competitor is usually "do nothing".** The previous
attempt question matters too — a failed internal build tells you what the
organization already believes about this problem, and often what killed the last
attempt will kill yours.

## Closing the call

Leave with four things:

1. **The metric**, and whose it is.
2. **The deadline** the pain is attached to.
3. **The name** of the next person you need to meet.
4. **A specific next step**, with a date, that the buyer agreed to.

If you have those four, discovery worked. If you have three, name the missing
one out loud before the call ends — people will usually fill it in when asked
directly, and reconstructing it later costs a whole extra call.

> "Before we wrap — what's the one thing that would stop this from happening?"

Asked at the end, when rapport is highest, this question is answered honestly
far more often than it has any right to be.
